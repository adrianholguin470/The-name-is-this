# If revenue doubled next quarter, what breaks first?

*By Adrian A. Holguin, Principal, Holguin & Company · holguincompany.com/principal*

Owners answer this question quickly. The answer is almost never the thing they came to talk about.

It is fulfillment capacity. It is one person who holds too much. It is a cash conversion cycle that gets worse as volume rises, so the better the quarter, the tighter the account. Growth does not fix a constrained business. It exposes it.

## The question is a diagnostic in one line

Ask it of a leadership team and watch where the eyes go. Sales looks at operations. Operations looks at the one person who approves everything. Finance looks at the collections report nobody has read since spring. The room has just told you where the constraint lives, and it did it in under a minute.

That is the whole point of a diagnostic: find the one thing actually holding the business back, with evidence, before spending money on the symptom.

## Three places it usually breaks

**Capacity that was never measured.** Most owner-led companies know their revenue to the dollar and their capacity to the feeling. "We can take on more" is a mood, not a number. Doubling revenue against unmeasured capacity produces late delivery, then discounting to apologize for it, then margin erosion that looks like a pricing problem and is not.

**A person who is the process.** One person holds the customer history, knows how the exception is handled, and signs off before the work moves. At current volume this looks like diligence. At double volume it is a queue. Document the exceptions, not just the normal path, and test the handoff with the backup person before you need it.

**Cash that lags the sale.** If customers pay in sixty days and suppliers want thirty, every new dollar of revenue borrows from the account for a month. Twice the revenue is twice the borrowing. The best quarter in the company's history can be the one where payroll gets tight.

## What to do with the answer

Write it down, name an owner, and give it a date. Then build the ninety-day plan around that one constraint, not around the ten improvements everyone wants. When the constraint moves, the next one shows itself. That is not failure. That is the business telling you where to work next.

You don't improve a company by attacking symptoms. You improve it by finding the constraint underneath them.
